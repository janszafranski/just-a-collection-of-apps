Part I
Getting Started
Chapter 1

Welcome to PAINTcanvas

PAINTcanvas is a raster image editor — a program for painting, retouching and adjusting pictures made of pixels. It belongs to the just a… family of apps and works in the tradition of Serif's PhotoPlus: paint with brushes, draw shapes, correct colour, apply filters, stack layers, cut a subject out from its background, and save a PNG or JPEG.

What a raster editor is

A picture on a screen is a grid of coloured dots called pixels. A raster (or bitmap) editor lets you change those pixels directly — darken them, blur them, paint new ones over the top. That is exactly how a photograph works, which is why raster editors are the natural home for retouching photos, painting digital artwork, and preparing images for a web page or a printed layout.

Its vector sibling, DRAWmaster, is different: there a circle stays a mathematical circle you can resize forever without blurring. In PAINTcanvas a circle is painted into the pixels — perfect for photographs, textures and painterly work, but it is the pixels, not shapes, that you edit from then on.

What you can do with it

PAINTcanvas gathers the everyday tools of a photo editor into one small window:

  • Paint and draw — a brush, an eraser, straight lines, rectangles, ellipses, a flood fill and a gradient;
  • Retouch — a clone (rubber-stamp) tool, red-eye removal, and dodge and burn for lightening and darkening by hand;
  • Adjust colour and tone — brightness and contrast, levels, hue and saturation, colour temperature and balance, and one-click fixes like auto-contrast, greyscale and sepia;
  • Apply filters — blur, sharpen, unsharp mask, despeckle, emboss, find-edges and more;
  • Work in layers — stack images, blend them, change their opacity;
  • Cut out a subject — the Cutout tool and Cutout Studio erase a background to transparency;
  • Add effects — a soft drop shadow or outer glow behind your artwork.
NoteAdjustments and filters are powered by the Pillow and NumPy libraries. If they are not installed, those menus explain how to add them; painting, shapes and layers work regardless.

The just a… family

PAINTcanvas has three companions, built to work together:

  • PAGEdesigner lays out printed pages — and hands a photo to PAINTcanvas for retouching, taking the result straight back;
  • WEBcreator builds websites the same way;
  • DRAWmaster is for vector illustration and logos.

They share the CREATORsuite launcher and a common Pictures/just a… folder, so a picture you save here is one click away in the others. A list of your recent files is shared too.

About this guide

This User Guide is the complete reference. It is organised in parts: the first gets you working; the middle teaches each group of tools in depth, with step-by-step procedures; then come worked projects you can follow start to finish, and finally a reference section — menus, shortcuts, a tool reference, a glossary and troubleshooting.

NoteThroughout, a numbered “To …” box is a procedure you can follow exactly. A Tip suggests a faster or better way. A Remember box flags something easy to forget.

For a gentler first read, start with the Quick Start Guide (Help ▸ Quick Start), which paints, adjusts and cuts out a picture in about a dozen short steps.

Conventions used here

A few conventions keep the instructions short:

  • Menu ▸ Command means “open this menu and choose this command”, e.g. Image ▸ Resize Image….
  • Keys are shown like Ctrl+S; on a Mac read Ctrl as Cmd.
  • Click means the left mouse button; drag means press, move, release.
  • Ctrl-click means hold Ctrl while you click — used to set the clone tool's source.
Chapter 2

Starting Out

This chapter covers the very first things: opening the program, starting a new picture (including a transparent one), opening an existing image, and saving your work as a PNG or JPEG.

Launching the program

Open just a… PAINTcanvas from the CREATORsuite launcher, from your system's application menu, or by running paint in a terminal. You can also start it on a picture directly — drag an image onto its icon, or pass the file on the command line — and it opens with that image loaded.

Starting a new picture

Choose File ▸ New… (Ctrl+N) to begin a fresh canvas.

To start a new picture

  1. Choose File ▸ New….
  2. Pick a size from the list — 1000×700, HD (1920×1080), a square, A4 at 150 dpi, or a 1080×1080 social-media square.
  3. Choose a background colour, or tick Transparent background for a canvas with no backdrop at all.
  4. Click Create.
TipA transparent background is the right choice for a logo, a cut-out, or anything you will drop on top of another picture later — the empty areas stay see-through when you save a PNG. The transparent squares you see are just a checkerboard marking “nothing here”.

Opening an existing image

To open a picture

  1. Choose File ▸ Open… (Ctrl+O).
  2. Browse to a PNG, JPEG, BMP, GIF or WebP file and choose it.
  3. The picture loads onto a single Background layer, ready to edit.
NotePAINTcanvas opens in your shared Pictures/just a… folder by default, so pictures saved by the other apps are easy to find.

Opening from your image library

If you keep a local stock-photo library, File ▸ Open from Library… lets you browse it by category and open a photo straight into PAINTcanvas — no hunting through folders.

To open a photo from your library

  1. Choose File ▸ Open from Library….
  2. Pick a category from the drop-down — each is one folder of your library (e.g. “Premium Image Collection 6 / People”).
  3. Click a thumbnail to choose that photo, then Open this photo.
  4. The picture loads onto a single Background layer, exactly as File ▸ Open would.
NoteThumbnails are decoded small and fast, so even a library of thousands of high-resolution photos scrolls smoothly. The library is read straight from disk — nothing is copied until you edit and save.
TipNo library yet? The same dialog has a Browse for an image… button to open any image file on your computer.

Saving your work

Save with File ▸ Save (Ctrl+S) or Save As…. PAINTcanvas writes two formats:

  • PNG — lossless, and it keeps transparency. Use it for artwork, logos, cut-outs and anything with sharp edges or see-through areas.
  • JPEG — smaller files, but no transparency and a little quality lost to compression. Use it for photographs you want to share or email.
RememberSaving a layered picture flattens all its layers into the one image in the file — PNG and JPEG cannot store separate layers. Keep a master PNG if you expect to re-edit, and remember that saving as JPEG fills any transparent areas with white.

Exporting to other formats

For anything beyond the everyday PNG and JPEG, File ▸ Export… (Ctrl+Shift+E) opens a dialog that gives you the choice of format and the controls that go with it.

The Format menu offers five formats, and the other controls enable or grey out to suit the one you pick:

  • PNG — lossless, with transparency. The Keep transparency box lets you flatten onto white instead if you prefer a solid picture.
  • JPEG — small photographic files; the Quality slider trades file size against compression, and there is no transparency.
  • WEBP — a modern web format that is smaller than both and keeps transparency; it has a Quality slider too.
  • GIF — a simple, widely-supported format (limited colours), with transparency.
  • BMP — an uncompressed bitmap for programs that need one.

As you change the format, quality or transparency, an Estimated size line updates so you can see roughly how big the file will be before you commit.

To export a picture

  1. Choose File ▸ Export….
  2. Pick the Format. The quality slider and transparency box enable themselves to suit it.
  3. For JPEG or WEBP, drag the Quality slider — watch the estimated file size change — and for PNG decide whether to Keep transparency.
  4. Click Export…, choose a name and place, and confirm.
TipExporting never touches your working picture — it only writes a copy in the chosen format. Use Save for your ordinary PNG/JPEG working file and Export when you need WEBP for the web, a GIF, or a tuned-down JPEG for email.
Chapter 3

The Workspace

Everything happens in one window: your picture in the middle, the tools down the left, the colour and size controls across the top, and the Layers panel on the right. This chapter names every part so the rest of the guide can refer to them.

A tour of the window

A new picture opens centred on a dark pasteboard, with everything you need around it.

The parts are:

  • the menu bar across the top — File, Edit, Image, Adjust, Filter, Select, Layer, Effects, Help;
  • the paint toolbar below it — the current colour, the brush size, and zoom;
  • the vertical tool palette down the left edge;
  • the picture itself in the centre, on its pasteboard;
  • the Layers panel on the right;
  • the status bar along the bottom, which shows hints and the cursor's pixel position.

The tool palette

The vertical tool palette on the left holds every tool you paint and edit with. Click one to select it; its name appears as a tooltip and in the status bar.

From the top: Brush, Eraser, Line, Rectangle, Ellipse, Fill (the bucket), Gradient, Pick colour (the eyedropper), Text, Clone, Red-eye, Dodge, Burn, the two marquee selection tools (rectangle and ellipse), the Magic Wand (select by colour), and the Cutout tool. Each is covered in the chapters that follow.

The paint toolbar

The horizontal toolbar below the menus carries the settings shared by most tools.

  • The Colour button shows and sets the current colour — click it for a full colour picker.
  • Size sets the brush/line/eraser width in pixels, from 1 to 200.
  • Zoom +, Zoom − and 100% change how large the picture appears on screen — without changing the picture itself.
TipZoom is just magnification for your eyes. To change the real pixel size of a picture, use Image ▸ Resize Image… instead.

The Layers panel

The Layers panel on the right stacks your picture's layers, topmost at the top, and controls each one. Layers get a chapter of their own; for now, note the parts.

  • A blend menu and an opacity slider at the top set how the active layer mixes with those below it;
  • the list shows every layer — tick its box to show or hide it, double-click to rename it, click to make it active;
  • the row of buttons adds, duplicates, merges down, moves up or down, and deletes a layer.

Moving around and zooming

To change the zoom

  1. Click Zoom + or Zoom − on the paint toolbar, or
  2. click 100% to return to actual size.
  3. Scroll bars appear automatically when the picture is larger than the window.

The status bar at the foot shows the cursor's position in pixels as you move over the picture — useful for placing something precisely, or reading off a size.

Undo, redo and the history

PAINTcanvas keeps a generous undo history, so you can paint and experiment freely.

To undo and redo

  1. Choose Edit ▸ Undo (Ctrl+Z) to step back, one change at a time.
  2. Choose Edit ▸ Redo (Ctrl+Shift+Z) to step forward again.
NoteThe history remembers the last two dozen changes. It is cleared when you start or open a new picture.
Part II
Painting, Retouching and Selecting
Chapter 4

Painting and Drawing

The painting tools put colour on the canvas directly: a freehand brush, an eraser, straight lines and shapes, a flood fill, and a gradient. Colour and size on the paint toolbar apply to them all.

Choosing a colour and size

To set the colour and brush size

  1. Click the Colour button on the paint toolbar and pick a colour (by swatch, by sliders, or by typing a hex value).
  2. Set the Size spinner to the width you want, in pixels.
  3. Both apply to the brush, eraser, line, rectangle, ellipse and gradient from now on.
TipTo match a colour already in the picture, use the Pick colour (eyedropper) tool and click the pixel you want — the colour becomes the current colour.

The Brush and Eraser

The Brush paints a soft round stroke in the current colour; drag to draw freehand. The Eraser works the same way but removes pixels instead — on a layer with transparency it erases to see-through; on the solid Background it reveals whatever is beneath.

To paint a freehand stroke

  1. Choose the Brush, set a colour and size.
  2. Press and drag on the canvas; release to end the stroke.
  3. Each stroke is one undo step, so a mistake is one Ctrl+Z away.

Lines, rectangles and ellipses

Three tools draw crisp shapes in the current colour and size. Each previews as you drag, so you see the result before you let go.

To draw a line or shape

  1. Choose the Line, Rectangle or Ellipse tool.
  2. Press where it should start and drag to where it should end.
  3. Release to paint it. The outline width follows the Size setting.

Filled shapes

When the Rectangle or Ellipse tool is chosen, a Shape menu and a Fill colour button appear on the paint toolbar. The menu offers three ways to draw the shape:

  • Outline — an unfilled outline in the current colour and size (the classic behaviour);
  • Fill — a solid shape with no outline;
  • Both — a filled shape and an outline around it.

By default the fill uses the current colour; click Fill colour to pick a different colour just for the fill, so you can outline in one colour and fill with another.

To draw a solid shape

  1. Choose the Rectangle or Ellipse tool.
  2. Set the Shape menu to Fill (solid) or Both (solid with an outline).
  3. Leave the fill as the current colour, or click Fill colour to choose a separate one.
  4. Drag out the shape and release. It is painted solid.
TipThe Line tool is always a stroke — only rectangles and ellipses have an interior to fill.

The Fill (bucket) tool

The Fill tool floods a connected area of one colour with the current colour — the classic paint bucket. Click inside a region and every touching pixel of the same colour changes at once.

To fill an area with colour

  1. Choose the Fill tool and set the colour you want.
  2. Click inside the area to flood.
  3. Only the connected same-coloured pixels change, so a shape’s outline keeps the fill contained.
TipFill works on exact colour matches. If a photo’s “flat” area is really many slightly different pixels, the fill may stop early — flatten the colour first with Posterize or Threshold, or paint by hand.

The Gradient tool

The Gradient tool paints a smooth two-colour blend across the canvas. When it is chosen, a Gradient label with Start and End colour buttons and a Fade to clear tick box appear on the paint toolbar. Pick a start colour and an end colour, then drag: the start of the drag is the start colour and the end of the drag is the end colour, blended evenly between. It is ideal for a sky wash, a two-tone background, or a coloured fade behind a subject.

To paint a two-colour gradient

  1. Choose the Gradient tool.
  2. Click Start and pick the first colour; click End and pick the second.
  3. Drag across the canvas in the direction you want the blend to run; release to paint it.
TipTick Fade to clear to fade the start colour out to transparent instead of to a second colour — the way to make a soft vignette or feather an overlay out at one edge. If a selection is active, the gradient is confined to it.

Adding text

The Text tool stamps a line of text into the picture as pixels.

To add a line of text

  1. Choose the Text tool and set the colour.
  2. Click where the text should start.
  3. Type the text in the little box that appears and confirm. It is painted at a size based on the brush size (roughly four times it).
RememberBecause the text becomes pixels immediately, you cannot re-edit the words afterwards — only paint over them or undo. For headline text you will keep tweaking, set it in PAGEdesigner or DRAWmaster instead, where text stays editable.
Chapter 5

Retouching

Four tools repair and refine a photograph by hand: the Clone stamp copies one part of a picture over another, Red-eye removal fixes flash-reddened eyes, and Dodge and Burn lighten and darken by brushing.

The Clone tool

The Clone (rubber-stamp) tool paints with pixels copied from somewhere else in the picture — the standard way to cover a blemish, remove an object, or extend a texture.

To clone out a blemish

  1. Choose the Clone tool.
  2. Hold Ctrl and click a clean area to set the source — the place to copy from.
  3. Let go of Ctrl and paint over the spot you want to hide. Pixels from the source are stamped under your brush, the source moving in step with you.
  4. Re-set the source (Ctrl-click) whenever you want to copy from somewhere new.
TipClone from an area with similar colour and texture, and keep strokes short — it looks most natural when the copied pixels blend into their surroundings.

Red-eye removal

Flash photography can turn eyes red. Red-eye removal neutralises it in one click.

To remove red-eye

  1. Choose the Red-eye tool.
  2. Set the Size large enough to cover the red part of the eye.
  3. Click on the red pupil. Strongly red pixels in a small area around the click are darkened to a natural grey, leaving the rest of the eye untouched.

Dodge and Burn

Dodge lightens and Burn darkens, applied by brushing — the darkroom technique for lifting a shadow or deepening a highlight a little at a time.

To lighten or darken an area by hand

  1. Choose Dodge to lighten or Burn to darken.
  2. Set a generous brush size.
  3. Brush gently over the area. The effect builds up as you go over it, so start light and repeat.
NoteDodge and burn are subtle on purpose — each pass changes the pixels only a little, so you keep control. For a big, even change, reach for Adjust ▸ Brightness / Contrast instead.
Chapter 6

Selecting (Magic Wand, Feather, Invert)

A selection fences off part of the picture so the next thing you do affects only that area. PAINTcanvas offers three ways to select — a rectangle marquee, an ellipse marquee and the Magic Wand (select by colour) — and a set of Select-menu commands to invert, feather, grow and shrink whatever you have selected. Every adjustment, filter, the Gradient tool and Crop respect the selection if one is active.

The marquee tools

To select a rectangular or oval area

  1. Choose the Select — rectangle or Select — ellipse tool from the tool palette.
  2. Drag across the picture to mark the area. A dashed outline shows the selection.
  3. Apply an adjustment or filter — it now affects only inside the marquee.
NoteThe dashed “marching ants” outline is a marker, not part of the picture; it never prints or saves.

The Magic Wand — select by colour

The Magic Wand (✦ in the tool palette) selects an area by its colour rather than by dragging a box. Click a pixel and it grows a selection outward to cover every neighbouring pixel of a similar colour — perfect for grabbing a sky, a flat background, or one coloured object.

To select an area by colour

  1. Choose the Magic Wand tool. A Wand tol (tolerance) box and a Contiguous checkbox appear in the top toolbar.
  2. Click a pixel in the colour you want. The matching region becomes the selection, shown as a soft blue tint with a bright outline.
  3. Raise Wand tol to catch more shades (a wider colour range); lower it to be stricter.
  4. Leave Contiguous ticked to select only the connected patch you clicked; untick it to select every matching pixel in the picture at once.
TipThe wand makes a free-form selection (a “mask”), not a box — so it follows the exact edge of whatever you clicked. Every adjustment, filter and the Gradient tool then apply only inside it.

Invert, Feather, Grow and Shrink

Once you have a selection, the Select menu reshapes it:

  • Invert (Ctrl+Shift+I) swaps selected for unselected — select a subject with the wand, invert, and you now have its background (ideal for blurring or darkening everything but the subject);
  • Feather… softens the selection edge by a radius you type, so the next adjustment fades smoothly in and out instead of stopping at a hard line;
  • Grow… expands the selection outward by a few pixels;
  • Shrink… pulls it inward.

To blend an adjustment at the edges with Feather

  1. Make a selection (wand or marquee).
  2. Choose Select ▸ Feather… and enter a radius in pixels — larger means a softer, wider fade.
  3. Apply an adjustment or filter. Instead of a crisp border, the effect now blends gradually across the feathered edge.
NoteFeather, Invert, Grow and Shrink all work on a free-form mask internally — applying any of them to a rectangle or ellipse marquee automatically converts it to a mask first, which is why the outline changes from a clean box to a traced edge.

Select All and Deselect

Two menu commands round out selecting:

  • Select ▸ Select All (Ctrl+A) selects the whole picture;
  • Select ▸ Deselect (Ctrl+D) clears the selection so changes affect everything again.
RememberIf an adjustment or filter seems to touch only part of the picture, you probably have a selection active. Deselect and try again.

What a selection affects

A selection confines the Adjust and Filter commands and the Gradient tool to its area, and Image ▸ Crop to Selection trims the whole picture down to it (for a wand/mask selection, down to its bounding box). The painting tools (brush, eraser, shapes) are not fenced in by a selection — they paint wherever you drag.

Part III
Adjustments and Filters
Chapter 7

Adjusting Colour and Tone

The Adjust menu corrects and transforms colour and brightness across the whole picture — or, if one is active, just the selection. Most open a dialog with a live preview; the one-click fixes at the foot of the menu apply at once.

Brightness and Contrast

The everyday fix for a photo that is too dark, too flat, or too washed out.

To brighten and add punch

  1. Choose Adjust ▸ Brightness / Contrast….
  2. Drag Brightness up to lighten the whole picture.
  3. Drag Contrast up to make darks darker and lights lighter — more “snap”.
  4. Click OK to keep the change, or Cancel to leave the picture as it was.

Levels

Levels gives finer control over the tonal range by setting the black point, the white point, and the mid-tone gamma.

To set the black and white points

  1. Choose Adjust ▸ Levels….
  2. Raise the Black point so the darkest pixels become true black.
  3. Lower the White point so the brightest become true white.
  4. Nudge Gamma to lighten or darken the mid-tones without moving the ends.
TipLevels is the most precise way to fix a dull, low-contrast photo: pull the black and white points inward to the edges of the picture’s real tonal range.

Curves

Curves is the most powerful tone and colour control of all. Instead of a few sliders it gives you a graph: the horizontal axis is the input brightness (shadows on the left, highlights on the right) and the vertical axis is the output. A straight diagonal changes nothing; bend it and you remap every tone at once.

To shape tone and colour with Curves

  1. Choose Adjust ▸ Curves….
  2. Click anywhere on the line to add a control point, then drag it. Pull the middle up to lighten, down to darken.
  3. For the classic “S-curve” that adds contrast, raise the upper part and lower the lower part — brightening highlights and deepening shadows.
  4. Switch the Channel box to Red, Green or Blue to shift a single colour channel (for example, lowering Blue in the shadows warms them).
  5. Right-click a point to remove it, or press Reset to return to the straight line. Click OK to apply.
TipCurves does everything Brightness, Contrast and Levels do, and more, in one place — but a gentle bend goes a long way. Like every adjustment it respects an active selection, so you can reshape the tones of just one area.

Hue and Saturation

Shift every colour around the wheel, and make colours more or less vivid.

To boost or shift colour

  1. Choose Adjust ▸ Hue / Saturation….
  2. Drag Hue shift to rotate all the colours — greens toward blue, reds toward orange, and so on.
  3. Drag Saturation up for richer colour, or all the way down toward grey.

Colour Temperature and Colour Balance

Two tools correct a colour cast.

To warm up or cool down a picture

  1. Choose Adjust ▸ Colour Temperature….
  2. Drag toward Warm to add orange (sunset, indoor light) or toward Cool to add blue (shade, overcast).

To remove a colour cast

  1. Choose Adjust ▸ Colour Balance….
  2. Nudge the three sliders — Cyan–Red, Magenta–Green, Yellow–Blue — until the cast is gone and neutral areas look neutral.

Threshold and Posterize

Two tools that simplify colour deliberately.

Threshold turns every pixel either black or white, split at a level you choose — a stark, high-graphic look, and a quick way to make a stencil or a line mask.

Posterize reduces each colour channel to a few levels (1–8), giving flat bands of colour like a screen-printed poster.

One-click fixes

The lower half of the Adjust menu applies at once, with no dialog:

  • Auto Contrast — stretches the tonal range automatically; the fastest first fix for a flat photo;
  • Equalize — spreads tones evenly for maximum contrast;
  • Greyscale — removes all colour, leaving a neutral black-and-white image;
  • Invert (Negative) — flips every colour to its opposite, like a film negative;
  • Sepia — a warm brown monochrome, for a vintage feel.
TipAll of these respect a selection — greyscale just one object in a colour photo by selecting it first, for a spot-colour effect.
Chapter 8

Filters

Filters transform the texture of a picture rather than its colour — softening, sharpening, cleaning up noise, or producing an artistic effect. Three open a dialog with a radius or size; the rest apply in one click. All honour an active selection.

Gaussian Blur

The all-purpose softening filter — for a dreamy background, a soft shadow, or to hide noise.

To soften a picture or a selection

  1. Choose Filter ▸ Gaussian Blur….
  2. Drag Radius up for more blur, watching the preview.
  3. Click OK. Select a background first to blur only it, keeping the subject sharp.

Unsharp Mask and Median

Unsharp Mask is the professional sharpener: it increases edge contrast by a radius you set, which makes a slightly soft photo look crisp without the harsh halos of a blunt sharpen.

Median (Despeckle) replaces each pixel with the median of its neighbours over a chosen size — superb at removing speckle and small blemishes while keeping edges reasonably clean.

One-click filters

The rest of the Filter menu applies instantly:

  • Sharpen — a quick edge boost;
  • Soften — a gentle smoothing;
  • Detail — lifts fine detail;
  • Emboss — turns the picture into a grey relief, as if pressed into metal;
  • Find Edges — traces the outlines on a dark ground, like a pen drawing;
  • Contour — a lighter line-art effect.
Part IV
Layers, Cutouts and Effects
Chapter 9

Layers

Layers are sheets of picture stacked one above another. Paint on one without disturbing the rest, slide a layer’s opacity, and choose how it blends with those below. The Layers panel on the right runs the whole stack.

Why layers help

Keeping parts of a picture on separate layers means you can change your mind. Put a caption on its own layer and you can move or delete it without touching the photo. Put a colour wash on a layer above and you can dial its strength down, or hide it, at any time.

Creating and managing layers

To add, duplicate and delete layers

  1. Click ＋ in the Layers panel (or Layer ▸ New Layer) for a new, transparent layer above the active one.
  2. Click ⧉ (or Layer ▸ Duplicate Layer) to copy the active layer.
  3. Click 🗑 (or Layer ▸ Delete Layer) to remove it — the last remaining layer cannot be deleted.
  4. Double-click a layer’s name to rename it.

To restack layers

  1. Click the ↑ and ↓ buttons to move the active layer up or down the stack.
  2. A layer higher in the list sits in front of those below it.

Showing, hiding and the active layer

The tick box beside each layer shows or hides it without deleting anything — handy for comparing a before and after, or temporarily getting a layer out of the way. Click a layer in the list to make it the active layer: that is the one every tool, adjustment and filter works on.

RememberA common surprise is painting and seeing nothing happen — because a different layer is active, or the active layer is hidden. Glance at the Layers panel to check which layer is selected and visible.

Opacity and blend modes

Two controls at the top of the panel change how the active layer mixes with the layers beneath.

  • Opacity — from 100% (solid) down to 0% (invisible). Lower it to let what is below show through.
  • Blend — how the layer’s colours combine with those below: Multiply darkens, Screen lightens, Overlay boosts contrast, and Darken, Lighten, Add and Difference give further effects.
TipA quick vignette or tint: add a layer, paint or fill it with colour, then try the Multiply or Screen blend and ease the opacity down until it looks right.

Overlays and textures

Two commands on the Image menu drop a ready-made picture straight onto a new layer for you, so you can blend it over your work without disturbing anything beneath. They are the quick way to add the kind of finishing effect Serif's PhotoPlus called an instant effect.

Image ▸ Add Overlay… adds an overlay — a light leak, a grunge or paper grain, a decorative frame, a drift of bokeh. The overlay arrives on its own layer, scaled to cover the canvas, and is set to the Screen blend at full strength by default so its bright marks glow over the picture while its dark areas drop away. Both the dialog's Browse button (for any image on disk) and a category + swatch grid reading your installed overlay library are offered.

To add an overlay

  1. Choose Image ▸ Add Overlay….
  2. Either click Browse for an image… and pick a file, or choose a category and click a swatch from your overlay library.
  3. Pick a Blend mode (Screen suits most light leaks and glows; try Overlay or Multiply for grunge and frames) and set the Opacity.
  4. Click Add to new layer. The overlay lands on its own layer, which you can then re-blend, fade, move or mask like any other.

Image ▸ Place Texture… works the same way over your texture library — paper, concrete, metal, fabric, wood and so on — but places the texture to cover the whole canvas at Normal / 100% so that you choose how to blend it. Set it to Multiply to let a grain or weave sink into the picture, or add a mask to show the texture only where you want it.

To place a texture

  1. Choose Image ▸ Place Texture….
  2. Browse for an image, or choose a category and click a swatch from your texture library.
  3. Click Add to new layer — the texture covers the canvas on a new layer at Normal / 100%.
  4. Now blend it: set the layer’s blend to Multiply or Overlay and ease the opacity down, or add a mask to reveal the texture only in places.
NoteThe overlay and texture libraries live outside the app, at ~/.local/share/justapps/overlays and ~/.local/share/justapps/textures, and are read live — nothing is copied in. If a library is not installed, the dialog still offers Browse so you can use your own images.

Merging and flattening

Layer ▸ Merge Down (or the ⌄ button) fuses the active layer into the one below, baking in its opacity and blend. Merge when you are happy with a combination and want to simplify the stack.

NoteSaving a picture flattens every layer into the single image the file holds. Keep a layered master as a PNG only if you will re-edit — remembering that even a PNG stores just the flattened result, not the separate layers. Merge deliberately while the layers are still live.

Layer masks

A layer mask hides parts of a layer without erasing them. Instead of rubbing pixels out with the eraser — which is permanent — a mask is a separate greyscale picture attached to the layer: where the mask is white the layer shows in full; where it is black the layer is hidden; greys in between make it partly see-through. Paint the mask and the layer fades in and out; paint it white again and the pixels come straight back. It is the non-destructive way to blend two layers, feather an edge, or try a composite you can always undo.

The mask controls live at the foot of the Layers panel: an Add Mask / From Selection / Delete Mask row, a small mask thumbnail, and an Edit mask tick box. The same commands are on the Layer menu.

To mask part of a layer away

  1. Select the layer in the Layers panel.
  2. Click Add Mask (or Layer ▸ Add Layer Mask). A white, reveal-everything mask is attached and Edit mask switches on.
  3. With Edit mask ticked, paint on the picture: the Brush paints the mask in the current colour’s shade (paint with black to hide, grey to half-hide), and the Eraser paints it white to reveal again.
  4. Untick Edit mask to go back to painting the layer’s pixels as normal.

To mask a layer to a selection

  1. Make a selection — a marquee, or the Magic Wand around the part you want to keep.
  2. Click From Selection (or Layer ▸ Add Mask From Selection).
  3. The layer is masked to the selection: the selected area stays visible, everything else is hidden — the quick way to drop a cut-out subject onto the layers below.
NoteLayer ▸ Apply Mask bakes the mask into the layer for good (hidden areas become transparent), then removes it. Delete Mask (or the panel button) simply throws the mask away and brings the whole layer back. Saving or merging also bakes masks in — the file holds the flattened, masked result.
TipA soft mask is the classic way to blend two photos: put one above the other, add a mask, and paint a soft black-to-white gradient across the join so one fades smoothly into the other.
Chapter 10

Cutting Out a Background

Removing a background to transparency — so a subject can sit on another picture, a coloured panel, or a web page — is one of the most-wanted jobs in any image editor. PAINTcanvas does it with the Cutout tool and Cutout Studio.

How cutout works

A cutout replaces background pixels with transparency — nothing at all, shown as a checkerboard. PAINTcanvas works by colour: you point at the background, and it erases the connected run of similar-coloured pixels, stopping where the colour changes sharply — at the edge of your subject.

NoteCutout works best when the background is a fairly even colour clearly different from the subject. A busy or similar-coloured background needs more clicks, a higher tolerance, or some hand-painting with the eraser.

The Cutout tool

To erase a background by clicking

  1. Choose the Cutout tool from the foot of the tool palette.
  2. Click a patch of background. The connected area of that colour is erased to transparency.
  3. Click any remaining patches of background to clear them too.
  4. If it eats into your subject, undo (Ctrl+Z) and lower the tolerance in Cutout Studio first.

Cutout Studio

Image ▸ Cutout Studio… opens a small panel that controls the Cutout tool and offers a one-click auto-remove.

To remove a background with Cutout Studio

  1. Choose Image ▸ Cutout Studio…. It picks the Cutout tool for you, adding transparency to the picture if it had none.
  2. Set the Tolerance: higher catches more shades of the background in one click; lower protects the fine edges of your subject.
  3. Click the background in the picture to erase it, patch by patch, or
  4. click Remove background from the edges now to clear in one go whatever colour touches the four edges of the picture.
  5. Close the Studio when the subject stands alone on transparency.
TipSave a cutout as a PNG to keep the transparency. Saved as JPEG, the see-through areas fill with white.

Tidying the edges

After an automatic cutout a few stray pixels often cling to the subject, or a thin halo of the old background remains. Switch to the Eraser, zoom in, set a small size, and clean them off by hand. For a background that is too varied for one tolerance, raise the tolerance and click each distinct region in turn, or paint the awkward parts away with the eraser.

Chapter 11

Smart Mask — cutting out tricky subjects

The Cutout tool works by colour, so it struggles with a busy background or wispy edges like hair and foliage. Smart Mask takes a different approach, borrowed from Vertus Fluid Mask: you simply scribble on what to keep and what to remove, and an edge-aware engine separates the subject from the background for you.

How Smart Mask works

You paint two kinds of rough scribble over the picture — green KEEP strokes over the subject and red REMOVE strokes over the background. You do not have to be neat or trace the edge; a few strokes in each area are enough. When you press Apply, the engine studies the colours and edges around your scribbles and works out the precise boundary for itself.

NoteSmart Mask uses OpenCV’s grabCut for the sharpest, Fluid-Mask-grade edges, and the installer adds OpenCV automatically. If it is not present the tool falls back to a built-in NumPy colour model — a little softer on cluttered backgrounds, but it needs nothing extra and always works.

Painting the scribbles

To mark your subject and background

  1. Choose the Smart Mask tool (the ✎ at the foot of the tool palette).
  2. Left-drag over the subject to paint green KEEP scribbles — a stroke or two through the middle is plenty.
  3. Right-drag over the background (or switch the top toolbar to Paint REMOVE and left-drag) to paint red REMOVE scribbles.
  4. Use the size box on the toolbar for a fatter or finer scribble brush, and Clear scribbles to start over.
TipScribbles float above the picture — they never paint on it. Add a few strokes in any area the first Apply got wrong, then Apply again; the engine refines the result from the extra guidance.

Applying — selection or cut-out

Press Apply… on the toolbar (or choose Image ▸ Smart Mask (cut out subject)…) to open the dialog and turn your scribbles into a mask.

To cut out a subject with Smart Mask

  1. Scribble KEEP over the subject and REMOVE over the background, then click Apply….
  2. Set the Edge feather — a few pixels softens the boundary for hair or fuzzy edges; leave it at zero for a crisp cut.
  3. Tick Set the subject as the selection to make the subject the active selection, so every adjustment, filter and the Crop command then affect only the subject.
  4. Tick Cut the subject out to a new layer to copy the subject onto its own transparent layer, leaving the original untouched.
  5. Click Apply. You can undo (Ctrl+Z) and try again with more scribbles at any time.
Note“Set as selection” and “Cut out to a new layer” are independent — tick either, both, or neither. The cut-out is non-destructive: the subject lands on a new layer called Cut-out and your original layers are left as they were.

Feathering tricky edges

Hair, fur and foliage never have a hard edge, so a razor-sharp mask around them looks cut out with scissors. The Edge feather slider blurs the mask boundary by a few pixels, fading the subject into whatever you place behind it — the difference between an obvious paste-up and a believable composite.

TipFeather little and often: 1–3 px suits most portraits. Too much feather eats into solid edges, so raise it only where the subject is genuinely soft.
Chapter 12

Effects

The finishing touches: a soft drop shadow or outer glow behind everything you have drawn to lift a logo or cut-out subject off the page, and a photographic lens flare dropped over the whole picture.

Drop Shadow

A drop shadow casts a soft, offset shadow of the drawing’s opaque pixels behind them, giving a sense of depth.

To add a drop shadow

  1. Choose Effects ▸ Drop Shadow….
  2. Set the Offset across and down — how far the shadow falls.
  3. Set the Blur for a soft or crisp edge, the Strength for how dark it is, and a Colour (black is usual).
  4. Click OK.

Outer Glow

An outer glow surrounds the drawing with a soft halo of colour — good for a highlight, a sticker edge, or making a subject pop against a dark background.

To add an outer glow

  1. Choose Effects ▸ Outer Glow….
  2. Set the Blur (how far the glow spreads), the Strength and a Colour.
  3. Click OK.

Lens flare

A lens flare drops a bright light source into the picture — the glowing core, radiating star spikes, a chain of hexagonal and circular lens reflections streaming across the frame, an optional rainbow diffraction ring and a soft halo — the kind of sunlight-in-the-lens look you see in photographs and films. It lives on Image ▸ Lens Flare… and is applied on its own layer, so the picture underneath is never altered.

The dialog shows a live preview of your picture with the flare composited over it. Drag the crosshair to place the light source, or type positions into the Light X / Light Y sliders.

To add a lens flare

  1. Choose Image ▸ Lens Flare….
  2. Pick a Preset to start — Subtle for a gentle glint, Sunny for a warm daylight flare, Anamorphic for the wide blue streak of a cinema lens, or Sci-fi for a bright, busy spread.
  3. Drag the crosshair on the preview to place the light (the upper third of the frame usually looks most natural).
  4. Tune Brightness and Size, and shift the Hue and Warmth to match the light in your photo.
  5. Turn individual elements on or off — Star spikes, Reflections, Rainbow ring, Halo and the Anamorphic streak.
  6. Click Add flare layer.
NoteThe flare arrives on a new layer set to the Screen blend, so only its bright parts show and the picture beneath stays intact. Lower that layer’s Opacity in the Layers panel to dial the whole effect back, or hide and delete the layer to remove it.

Working on transparency

RememberThe drop shadow and outer glow show through transparent areas, so they only look right on a layer that has transparency around the artwork. Start from File ▸ New with a Transparent background, or cut the backdrop away first — otherwise the shadow or glow has nowhere to show. (The lens flare is different: it floats over whatever is beneath it, so it works on any picture.)

So the natural workflow is: make or cut out a subject on transparency, then apply the shadow or glow, then place the result on its new background.

Chapter 13

Resizing, Cropping and Rotating

The Image menu changes the picture as a whole: its pixel dimensions, its canvas, its orientation. These are the housekeeping jobs you do before output.

Resize Image vs. Canvas Size

Two commands sound alike but do opposite things.

To resize the whole picture

  1. Choose Image ▸ Resize Image….
  2. Type a new width; the height is suggested to keep the proportions.
  3. Type the height and confirm. Every pixel — and every layer — is scaled to the new size.

To change the canvas without scaling

  1. Choose Image ▸ Canvas Size….
  2. Type a new width and height.
  3. The picture keeps its pixels at their current size; the canvas grows (adding transparent margin) or shrinks (cropping) around them.
NoteResize Image makes the picture itself bigger or smaller. Canvas Size changes the area you have to work on, leaving the existing pixels untouched — use it to add room around a subject.

Cropping to a selection

To crop to a selection

  1. Choose a marquee tool and drag out the area you want to keep.
  2. Choose Image ▸ Crop to Selection.
  3. The picture is trimmed to the selection’s bounds, across every layer.

Rotating and flipping

The Image menu turns and mirrors the whole picture:

  • Rotate 90° Right / Left and Rotate 180° — quarter and half turns, for a photo shot sideways;
  • Flip Horizontal and Flip Vertical — mirror the picture.

Straightening

Image ▸ Straighten… rotates by a small angle and crops back to the original size — the fix for a tilted horizon.

To straighten a crooked photo

  1. Choose Image ▸ Straighten….
  2. Enter a small angle (between −45° and 45°).
  3. The picture rotates by that amount and is trimmed to fill the original frame, so no transparent corners are left.
Chapter 14

Worked Project — Retouch and Share a Photo

A complete pass over a photograph, from import to a finished JPEG, using the skills from the earlier chapters in the order you would really use them.

1 · Open and straighten

To start the project

  1. Choose File ▸ Open… and load the photo.
  2. If the horizon is tilted, use Image ▸ Straighten… with a degree or two.
  3. Crop in if you like: drag a rectangle marquee and choose Image ▸ Crop to Selection.

2 · Correct the tone and colour

To fix exposure and colour

  1. Try Adjust ▸ Auto Contrast first — it fixes many photos instantly.
  2. If it needs more, open Adjust ▸ Levels… and pull the black and white points to the edges of the tonal range.
  3. Warm or cool the picture with Adjust ▸ Colour Temperature…, and lift the colour with Hue / Saturation… if it looks flat.

3 · Retouch and sharpen

To clean up and finish

  1. Remove any blemish or stray object with the Clone tool (Ctrl-click a clean source, then paint).
  2. Fix any red-eye with the Red-eye tool.
  3. Finish with Filter ▸ Unsharp Mask… for a crisp result — a small radius is usually enough.

4 · Save and share

To export the finished photo

  1. Choose File ▸ Save As….
  2. Pick JPEG for a small, shareable file (fine for a photo with no transparency).
  3. Keep a PNG master too if you might re-edit — it loses no quality.
TipNeed the photo in a newsletter or on a web page? Save it, then open PAGEdesigner or WEBcreator — it is waiting in your shared Pictures/just a… folder and your recent files.
Chapter 15

Worked Project — A Cut-Out Logo on Transparency

Building a reusable graphic with a transparent background and a soft shadow — the kind of asset you drop onto a page, a banner or a slide.

1 · Start transparent

To begin on a clear canvas

  1. Choose File ▸ New…, pick a size, and tick Transparent background.
  2. The canvas opens as a checkerboard — nothing but transparency.

2 · Paint the mark

To draw the artwork

  1. Use the Brush, the shape tools and the Fill bucket to build your mark.
  2. Work on one or more layers so parts stay independent.
  3. Add a line of Text if the logo needs a word.

3 · (Or) cut a subject out of a photo

To reuse a photographed object instead

  1. Open a photo of the object on a plain background.
  2. Open Image ▸ Cutout Studio…, set a tolerance and click the background — or use Remove background from the edges.
  3. Tidy the edges with the Eraser at a small size.

4 · Add a shadow and save

To finish and save

  1. Choose Effects ▸ Drop Shadow… (or Outer Glow…) — because the surround is transparent, it shows correctly.
  2. Save as a PNG to keep the transparency.
  3. Drop the PNG into PAGEdesigner, WEBcreator or DRAWmaster; the clear areas let whatever is behind show through.

Appendix D — Glossary

The image-editing words used in this guide.

A–Z

Alpha / transparency
A pixel’s see-through-ness. Fully transparent pixels show as a checkerboard and let whatever is behind show through.
Blend mode
The rule for how a layer’s colours combine with those below — Multiply, Screen, Overlay and so on.
Canvas
The working area your picture occupies, measured in pixels.
Clone
Painting with pixels copied from another part of the picture.
Cutout
Removing a background to transparency, isolating a subject.
Flatten
Merging all layers into one image — what happens when you save.
Gamma
The mid-tone brightness control in Levels.
Gradient
A smooth fade between two colours (or from a colour to transparent) across an area.
Layer
One sheet of a stacked picture, editable on its own.
Layer mask
A greyscale picture attached to a layer that hides it (black) or reveals it (white) without erasing any pixels.
Marquee
A selection outline — the “marching ants”.
Opacity
How solid a layer or stroke is, from 100% to 0%.
Pixel
One coloured dot; the atom of a raster picture.
Posterize
Reducing colour to a few banded levels.
Raster / bitmap
A picture made of a grid of pixels.
Threshold
Forcing every pixel to pure black or white at a chosen level.
Tolerance
How wide a range of colours a fill or cutout treats as “the same”.
Unsharp mask
A sharpening filter that increases edge contrast.
Chapter 20

Appendix E — Troubleshooting

Quick answers to common snags.

Common questions

I paint but nothing appears.
A different layer is probably active, or the active layer is hidden. Check the Layers panel — click the layer you mean to paint on and make sure its tick box is on.
An adjustment or filter only changed part of the picture.
You have a selection active. Choose Select ▸ Deselect (Ctrl+D) and try again.
The Fill bucket leaves specks or stops too soon.
Fill matches an exact colour; a “flat” photo area is really many near-colours. Posterize or threshold it first, or paint by hand.
My cutout ate into the subject.
The tolerance is too high. Undo, lower the tolerance in Cutout Studio, and click again.
The transparent areas went white when I saved.
You saved as JPEG, which has no transparency. Save as PNG to keep it.
The drop shadow or glow doesn’t show.
The effect shows through transparency only. Start from a transparent background, or cut the backdrop away first.
The Adjust and Filter menus say they need libraries.
They use Pillow and NumPy. Install them with your package manager (e.g. sudo pacman -S python-pillow python-numpy); painting and layers work without them.
